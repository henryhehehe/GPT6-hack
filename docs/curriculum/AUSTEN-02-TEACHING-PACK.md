## Before teaching

Preview all three cards and decide whether to read them aloud, in pairs, or independently. The names needed here are supplied by the passages: Elizabeth receives and rereads Darcy’s letter; the rereading includes an account concerning Wickham. Do not require a relationship history, an absent witness statement, or knowledge of the ending.

Prepare a three-column organizer: **wording / what it suggests / what it cannot establish**. All core tasks below can be answered using the displayed cards. The optional longer context in the app is additional reading, not a prerequisite for a passing answer.

The garden, lane, desk, and fictional companions help organize the exercise. They are not evidence of Elizabeth’s mood, the exact location of each reading moment, or the truth of either account.

## Student worksheet

1. **Before reading:** What would count as a change of judgment, and what further evidence would be needed to establish that the new judgment is correct? A provisional answer or a question is enough.
2. **First reading:** Copy a short phrase from card 1. Explain what it suggests about conviction or emotion.
3. **Rereading:** Copy a short phrase from card 2. Compare its language of attention or effort with card 1. What changes?
4. **Self-assessment:** Select one or two words from card 3. Explain their effect in the list; do more than repeat that she feels bad.
5. **First interpretation:** Answer the inquiry using at least two cards. Identify whose belief or judgment you are discussing.
6. **Teacher challenge:** Which phrase supports your account of the change, rather than just naming it?
7. **Revision:** Explain a change in interpretation through wording and acknowledge an alternative reading. Cite the wording that prompted a change, or explain why your initial position remains defensible after the challenge.
8. **Reflection:** “I first thought… After comparing… I now think… / I still think… because… One limit is…” Do not invent a change merely to satisfy the worksheet.

## Vocabulary support

These are teaching explanations, not quotations or replacement source text. Keep the original wording available.

| Wording | Useful sense in this passage |
| --- | --- |
| contrariety of emotion | conflicting feelings |
| apology | an explanation or defense; do not assume the modern sense of simply saying sorry |
| perturbed | unsettled or troubled |
| mortifying | humiliating or painful to one’s pride |
| perusal | reading or examination |
| partial | biased or favoring one side, rather than incomplete |

For support, offer: “The phrase ___ suggests ___ because ___. Compared with ___, it changes ___. It does not prove ___.” For extension, ask whether the same wording supports both deliberate reconsideration and emotional self-reproach. Neither interpretation wins by label alone.

## Teacher feedback and assessment

Assess an interpretive claim, accurate supplied wording, analysis of how the wording supports the claim, and an alternative reading or source limit. A source ID or copied phrase alone is insufficient. Give a concrete next question rather than completing the argument for the learner. A changed answer is a revision submitted, not automatic evidence of improved reasoning.

The examples below are authored expectations, not observed model responses or mandatory scores.

### Supported interpretation: deliberate-attention

**Learner:** The narration presents reconsideration as effort, not just a new feeling. “steadfastly was she persuaded” gives way to an attempt to “examine the meaning of every sentence.” The later self-criticism shows a changed view of herself, but it does not independently prove every claim in Darcy’s letter.

**Feedback focus:** Recognize the contrast in wording and the limit on what self-assessment proves. Ask the learner to explain the final card’s adjective list if the interpretation needs development.

### Supported alternative: self-reproach

**Learner:** The change may be thoughtful, but it is also emotionally severe. “collecting herself as well as she could” suggests effort under strain, while “blind, partial, prejudiced, absurd” turns her criticism onto herself. I would not treat the intensity of that self-reproach as a guarantee of accurate judgment.

**Feedback focus:** Accept this qualified alternative if the learner explains the quoted details. Do not require a verdict that Elizabeth has become completely impartial or entirely correct.

### Needs revision: certainty-leap

**Learner:** Elizabeth is ashamed, so Darcy has been proved right about everything and she will never misjudge anyone again.

**Feedback focus:** Ask which words prove every claim or predict every later judgment. Keep the narrower observation that the narration represents shame and reassessment; remove unsupported certainty and future claims.

### Needs revision: scenery-leap

**Learner:** The garden and writing desk look peaceful, so Elizabeth must read calmly and understand everything accurately.

**Feedback focus:** The illustrated setting is not textual evidence. Ask the learner to compare that impression with conflicting emotion and effort in the cards, and support the revised interpretation with wording.

### First reading · `1342-1196-0`

*Chapter 36 · exact normalized source excerpt*

> ELIZABETH, when Mr. Darcy gave her the letter, did not expect it to contain a renewal of his offers, she had formed no expectation at all of its contents. But such as they were, it may be well supposed how eagerly she went through them, and what a contrariety of emotion they excited. Her feelings as she read were scarcely to be defined. With amazement did she first understand that he believed any apology to be in his power; and steadfastly was she persuaded, that he could have no explanation to give, which a just sense of shame would not conceal.

### The letter unfolded again · `1342-1198-0`

*Chapter 36 · exact normalized source excerpt*

> In this perturbed state of mind, with thoughts that could rest on nothing, she walked on; but it would not do: in half a minute the letter was unfolded again; and collecting herself as well as she could, she again began the mortifying perusal of all that related to Wickham, and commanded herself so far as to examine the meaning of every sentence. The account of his connection with{255} the Pemberley family was exactly what he had related himself; and the kindness of the late Mr. Darcy, though she had not before known its extent, agreed equally well with his own words.

*Chapter 36 · exact normalized source excerpt*
