## 1. Alexandria — Could knowledge survive a trade collapse?

**Catalog ID:** `alexandria-01`. **Subject:** history. **Suggested duration:** 45 minutes. **Prerequisite:** distinguish a source from a hypothesis. **Objective:** construct a conditional explanation, show which links rely on assumptions, and consider another explanation.

**Driving question:** Under which assumptions would less trade reduce scholar support?

The existing app provides the setting and three cards. The new teaching sequence below still needs explicit prediction, selected evidence, and revision support or a teacher-managed journal. This is an inquiry into a hypothetical system; it does not explain a documented destruction of the Library.

| Stop | Material and provenance | Student action | Evidence limit |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Harbor | Existing `ledger` card: invented ship counts | Record the change and label it an invented teaching prop | The counts cannot establish an ancient event |
| Market | Existing `funding` card: explicit teaching assumption | Draw the proposed connection between revenue and support; mark it conditional | The mechanism is stipulated rather than established by the source |
| House of knowledge | Existing `strabo` paraphrase; compare the approved excerpt of Geography 17.1.8 | Identify what the description supports about an institution | It does not quantify a library budget or make trade its sole support |

Source: [Strabo, Geography 17.1.8, Jones translation](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Strabo/17A1*.html). The source card and the client’s separate excerpt are not interchangeable canonical text. Resolve the authoritative passage before enabling exact quotation selections, as required by the implementation plan.

**Historical framing:** identify the Mouseion as a scholarly institution, distinguish it from the Library/book collections, and situate Strabo's account under Roman rule. The scene is illustrative. The [source review](HISTORICAL-ACCURACY-REVIEW.md) explains the distinction and its evidence limits.

**Teaching sequence.** Start with a one-sentence prediction and a confidence explanation. Students visit the three stops in any order and classify each material. They write a short argument using the source and explicitly named assumptions. The teacher responds to an actual sentence in that argument. Students then revise it and record which evidence or assumption changed their reasoning. A source list without a relevance explanation is incomplete.

**A plausible initial student claim for rehearsal:** “Fewer ships means the library must close.” This is an invented example, not a stored learner result.

**Teacher steering prompt:** “Ask the selected learner to identify the unsupported link in their explanation. Use the approved Strabo card and funding assumption. Ask about an alternative patron without asserting that such a patron appeared. Preserve the learner’s freedom to defend a different supported conclusion.” Applied hints are currently class-wide; do not describe this as a private intervention.

**Revision task:** “Rewrite your explanation so a reader can distinguish the source, the assumed mechanism, and the conditions under which your conclusion would fail. Explain one change from your first answer.”

**Assessment evidence:** a bounded claim; a traceable source reference with relevance; a conditional mechanism; and an alternative or limitation. A student may argue that the source packet is insufficient to predict closure and still produce a strong answer. Merely repeating the replacement-patron suggestion is insufficient unless the student explains its effect on the proposed mechanism.

**Optional branch:** hold harbor disruption constant and propose replacement funding. The scene comparison is labeled hypothetical; returning to baseline restores the original assumed state. No branch creates new historical evidence.

**Review checks:** the learner never cites the ledger as an ancient record; a source-absence claim is not confused with proof of survival; all three cards remain accessible; a teacher can compare the initial and revised sentences. A visible change in text is a revision submission, not automatically a learning gain.

## 2. The Odyssey — The name shouted back

**Catalog ID:** `odyssey-ix-02`. **Subject:** literature. **Suggested duration:** 45 minutes. **Prerequisite:** students have the teacher-approved Book IX packet. **Objective:** interpret leadership through contrasting choices and the language of a first-person account.

**Driving question:** How does revealing an identity change a reading of leadership?

Use [Samuel Butler’s prose translation, Book IX](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/1727/pg1727-images.html). Keep “Ulysses” in this edition’s quotations and displayed character naming, with an introductory note that the work is commonly discussed using “Odysseus.” Citation locators are book and stable passage ID, not fabricated verse numbers. Exclude the translator’s preface and later books from the approved model packet.

| Teaching stop | Passage in the current packet | Student action | Why this stop matters |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Shore briefing | `1727-419-0`: the narrator's inward boast about a stratagem | Separate the narrator's evaluation from the plan itself, which is absent here | Grounds analysis of self-presentation |
| Cave evidence station | `1727-426-0`: crew warning during departure | Explain the danger the crew describes | This is a reading station, not the passage's literal location |
| Departing ship | `1727-427-0`: refusal to listen and revelation of identity | Compare the decision with the crew's warning | Supplies a complication without prescribing one moral |

The complete assumed-name/escape plan and narrative-audience framing are not supplied in this lesson's bounded context. Review and add those passages before requiring a comparison of the actual escape mechanism or identification of the audience.

The shore, cave station, and ship are interpretive staging, not identified archaeological locations. A symbolic coast scene is a possible prototype after source review; an enterable cave or detailed native ship is not implemented merely by listing these stops.

**Teaching sequence.** Students predict whether a narrator's claim of cleverness is sufficient evidence of good leadership. They read the approved passages, select two contrasting details, and explain why each matters. An initial argument must address an action and how it is narrated. After the challenge, students revise and identify the detail that made their earlier description less adequate.

**Teacher steering prompt:** “Ask the learner to compare a detail supporting cleverness with the crew’s objection. Direct attention to the narrator’s wording. Do not supply a verdict about pride, require condemnation, or introduce any event after Book IX.”

**Revision task:** “Defend a reading of leadership that explains the narrator's claim of cleverness and his response to the crew's warning. Address another plausible reading and explain why your selected language supports your conclusion.”

**Assessment evidence:** an interpretive claim; two relevant selected passages; analysis of wording, perspective, or contrast; and a substantive alternative reading. Do not grade on whether the student chooses ‘heroic’ or ‘reckless.’ An unsupported quotation or a movie detail cannot count as textual evidence.

**Optional branch:** after the canonical reading, students may explore withholding the name. They must separate what the text establishes from an invented possibility. No branch claims to reveal the author’s unwritten ending; switching back restores canonical material. This branch is unnecessary for completing the close-reading lesson.

**Review checks:** displayed names match the translation; the student can read the full approved context; fabricated quotations fail verification; later-book spoiler requests do not enter model context; a well-supported favorable or critical reading can satisfy the rubric. Prepare source-aware reading guides first; simulated character dialogue is optional and visibly invented.

## 3. Pride and Prejudice — Reading again

**Catalog ID:** `austen-letter-02`. **Subject:** literature. **Suggested duration:** 45 minutes. **Prerequisite:** a teacher-approved introduction sufficient to locate Chapter 36 without later spoilers. **Objective:** explain reconsideration through narration and distinguish the recipient's reasoning from independent corroboration.

**Driving question:** How does the narration represent Elizabeth’s reconsideration?

Source: [Jane Austen, Pride and Prejudice, selected edition](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/1342/pg1342-images.html). The world permits Chapters 35–36, but this lesson supplies only selected Chapter 36 narration and bounded context. It reports Elizabeth's response to the letter; it does not supply a direct Chapter 35 letter passage. Additional passages require inclusion and review before comparison. Later chapters remain outside the boundary.

| Teaching stop | Passage in the current packet | Student action | Why this stop matters |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Hunsford lane | `1342-1196-0`: narration of the first reading | Identify expectations and resistance in the wording | Gives the first interpretation a textual basis |
| Letter-reading place | `1342-1198-0` and bounded context: rereading | Trace reported comparison, hesitation, and attempts at impartiality | Analyzes the reception of the letter without inventing its wording |
| Comparison desk | `1342-1205-0`: narrated self-assessment | Explain the change in Elizabeth's view of herself | Self-reproach is evidence of reassessment, not proof of every assertion in the letter |

Hunsford must not be relabeled Pemberley. The desk is a teaching station, not a claim that Austen staged the reading at that desk. The setting should provide readable comparisons rather than hide sources behind tiny letter textures.

**Teaching sequence.** Students make a provisional interpretation from the first reading passage, then compare the narration of rereading and self-assessment. They distinguish the narrator's presentation from Elizabeth's reported beliefs. The teacher challenges one leap in the student’s reasoning. Students revise their own interpretation and compare that process with the narrated reconsideration, without needing to endorse Elizabeth’s final judgment as correct.

**Teacher steering prompt:** “Ask the learner to compare the narration of Elizabeth's first reading and rereading. Ask which wording supports a change in interpretation and what remains uncertain. Accept a defensible skeptical reading; do not invent absent letter wording, reveal later corroboration, or prescribe an approved judgment.”

**Revision task:** “Explain how the wording represents a change in judgment. Identify one claim the packet supports and one uncertainty it leaves open. Compare your first and revised interpretations and explain the role of a selected passage.”

**Assessment evidence:** a focused interpretation; relevant Chapter 36 passages; analysis of narrative language; and an alternative reading. Treat ‘she changes her mind’ as a starting observation that needs analysis. Supported uncertainty can be an intellectually successful outcome. A direct letter/response comparison requires an additional reviewed Chapter 35 selection.

**Optional extension:** compare an earlier teacher-approved passage in a newly reviewed packet. An invented information-withholding exercise must retain canonical wording and be labeled as a thought experiment. Do not silently hide assigned evidence to force an interpretation.

**Review checks:** the packet does not leak later chapters; the source reader distinguishes letter text from narration; a strong skeptical interpretation can meet the rubric; no unsupported corroboration earns credit; the learner can explain a specific change in their own reasoning.

## Support and delivery for all three briefs

Offer labeled vocabulary notes, a visible source/claim/reasoning organizer, and keyboard-accessible station choices. Students may begin with a shorter claim and expand it during revision. Keep original excerpts available alongside any explanation. Invite extension through a competing interpretation rather than more object collection. Suggested lengths and timing are adjustable teaching choices, not validated thresholds.

Until product support is verified, a teacher-managed journal can hold prediction, initial answer, challenge, selected passage references, revised answer, and reflection. This supports rehearsal of the teaching design but does not establish that the app persists those fields. The complete product loop still requires the checks in [PRODUCT-READINESS.md](../PRODUCT-READINESS.md).
