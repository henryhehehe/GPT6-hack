## Before teaching

Ulysses is recounting the episode: quoted crew speech and his own self-description reach the reader through that account. The first card praises a stratagem while the Cyclops still guards the exit; it does not say the escape is complete at that moment. The second reports an earlier rock throw and forecasts further danger. The third narrates a refusal and a shout. These distinctions are sufficient context for this lesson.

Preview references to injury and threatened violence. Offer quiet reading or teacher read-aloud; no reenactment is needed. Students may answer in speech or a three-column organizer (**wording / interpretation / limit**) using card IDs. The scene, costumes and simulated companions are interpretive teaching material, not additional textual evidence.

## Assigned source cards

### 1. A successful stratagem — `1727-419-0`

> “Then they went away, and I laughed inwardly at the success of my clever stratagem, but the Cyclops, groaning and in an agony of pain, felt about with his hands till he found the stone and took it from the door; then he sat in the doorway and stretched his hands in front of it to catch anyone going out with the sheep, for he thought I might be foolish enough to attempt this.

### 2. The crew’s warning — `1727-426-0`

> “‘Do not,’ they exclaimed, ‘be mad enough to provoke this savage creature further; he has thrown one rock at us already which drove us back again to the mainland, and we made sure it had been the death of us; if he had then heard any further sound of voices he would have pounded our heads and our ship’s timbers into a jelly with the rugged rocks he would have heaved at us, for he can throw them a long way.’

### 3. The choice to speak — `1727-427-0`

> “But I would not listen to them, and shouted out to him in my rage, ‘Cyclops, if any one asks you who it was that put your eye out and spoiled your beauty, say it was the valiant warrior Ulysses, son of Laertes, who lives in Ithaca.’

## Teaching sequence

| Minutes | Stage | Activity |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 5 | Predict | Distinguish solving a danger from deciding what to do after an apparent success. Write a provisional question about leadership. |
| 8 | Read the self-description | Read card 1. Mark the first-person evaluation of the stratagem and identify the danger that remains in the same card. |
| 9 | Read the objection | Read card 2. Separate the crew’s reported experience from its forecast. Specify whether the warning concerns a name, further sound, or both. |
| 9 | Compare the response | Read card 3. Compare refusal, emotion and heroic self-description with cards 1–2. Explain a tension rather than choosing a single character label. |

| 4 | Exit check | State one supported inference and one consequence that these three cards do not establish. |

## Student worksheet

1. Before reading, pose a question about whether a successful tactic settles a judgment about leadership.
2. In card 1, copy a short evaluative phrase. Who evaluates the stratagem? What danger remains?
3. In card 2, separate the reported rock throw from the forecast about further voices. What reason does the crew offer for its warning?
4. In card 3, mark the refusal, the stated emotion and the self-description. Explain how two of these details work together or create tension.
5. Write an interpretation using at least two cards. Explain the wording rather than merely calling the speaker clever, proud or reckless.
6. **Teacher challenge:** What does the crew’s objection make harder to explain?
7. Revise the leadership claim without reducing the episode to a single trait. Identify the wording that changed your reading, or explain why it still supports your first interpretation after considering an alternative.
8. Exit: distinguish the risk of making further sound from a proposed consequence of revealing a particular identity. Which can these cards establish, and which needs more evidence?

Vocabulary explanations are teaching glosses: **stratagem** means a planned trick or tactic; **valiant** presents someone as brave; **timbers** are the ship’s structural wood. Keep the original words available. Do not replace analysis with these definitions.

## Formative comparisons

The following student responses are invented teaching examples. They are not source passages, real learner submissions, or observed model outputs. Expected feedback allows supported disagreement and does not prescribe a single correct interpretation.

### mixed-leadership — supported interpretation

**Invented student response:** His tactical cleverness coexists with poor attention to the crew. Calling the stratagem clever praises his success, but the later refusal to listen dismisses an objection based on an actual rock throw. His anger complicates the heroic title he gives himself.

**Expected feedback:** Accept the mixed interpretation. Ask how the contrast between inward laughter and shouted self-naming changes the presentation of success. Do not require a claim that the name itself causes later losses.

**Revision target:** Explain the effect of the contrasting forms of self-presentation.

### candor-with-limits — supported alternative

**Invented student response:** The narrated admission of refusing advice allows a reader to criticize the speaker. That admission makes the account more self-exposing than the heroic title alone suggests. It does not prove that the whole narrative is reliable or that the poet approves of the choice.

**Expected feedback:** Accept this alternative if the student analyzes the first-person admission. Ask for comparison with the earlier self-praise; do not require the same conclusion as the first example.

**Revision target:** Distinguish an effect of the narration from a claim about the narrator’s complete reliability.

### trait-label — plausible but underdeveloped

**Invented student response:** Ulysses is proud and therefore a bad leader. He calls himself valiant.

**Expected feedback:** The quoted self-description is relevant, but a label does not explain the decision. Ask the learner to compare it with the crew’s warning and the explicit refusal. Anger is also named; consider how the two possible motivations differ.

**Revision target:** Replace a single-trait verdict with a comparison supported by at least two cards.

### invented-order — deliberately fabricated quotation — diagnostic only

**Invented student response:** The crew orders, “Tell him your name so he knows our victory.” Therefore Ulysses follows their plan.

**Expected feedback:** Identify the invented sentence as absent from the packet. Return to the crew’s objection and his refusal. Invite a new claim rather than crediting a clear conclusion that depends on false attribution.

**Revision target:** Replace the invented quotation with exact assigned wording and reconsider the conclusion.

### later-losses — out-of-packet consequence

**Invented student response:** His name makes Neptune destroy the entire crew later, so these cards prove that cleverness is useless.

**Expected feedback:** These three cards do not contain a prayer, a divine response, or later deaths. Do not supply later events as assessment evidence. Ask what the crew’s warning and the narrated decision establish here.

**Revision target:** Limit the consequence claim to the supplied warning and decision; mark later outcomes as unestablished by this packet.

### precise-warning — warranted distinction

**Invented student response:** The crew warns about further voices after a rock has already been thrown. That establishes a danger in speaking again, not a separate causal effect of announcing this particular name. The heroic title can still matter to how we interpret the decision.

**Expected feedback:** Accept the distinction. Ask how naming and heroic self-description affect interpretation without converting the crew’s sound warning into a claim about magical consequences.

**Revision target:** Preserve the difference between physical risk in the warning and the meaning of self-naming.

## Assessment and readiness

Assess an interpretive claim, accurate use of textual evidence, analysis of language or narrative, and an alternative reading or warranted limit. A student may argue that the episode undermines leadership, presents conflicting capabilities, or exposes the narrator’s self-presentation. Require reasons from the assigned wording, not agreement with one verdict. Neither a fabricated quotation nor a conclusion dependent on it earns credit merely because it sounds confident.

A useful revision explains what the comparison changes. Copying more quotation without connecting it to the claim is insufficient. A defensible unchanged claim with a reasoned response to the challenge is acceptable. Do not assess external mythology, unseen chapters, or visual realism.
