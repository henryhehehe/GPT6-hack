# Museum investigations: classroom guide

Ten reviewed object pairs, one for each curriculum topic. These are editorial discussion activities, not museum quotations or generated answers.

## Open and teach a pair

1. Open the museum collection and choose a topic, or use one of the links below. Three suggestions are initially visible when browsing all topics; choose **Show all 10 investigations** to expand the list. Topic filters show the matching investigation.
2. Choose **Explore this pair** to compare the photographs and read the timed prompts. Each image retains its own scale, so compare physical size using the museum dimensions.
3. In the classroom, **Add pair to lesson** saves both objects together, or adds the missing object. The six-object classroom limit still applies.
4. Learners see the guide when both objects are assigned. Complete pairs also appear in the teacher guide, with a printable worksheet and blank response spaces. Choose one eight-minute discussion within the lesson’s existing investigation time.

## Investigations

### Alexandria: How does power make itself visible?

[Open the comparison](http://localhost:5173/collections?study=royal-power) · 8 minutes

Two coins. Two portraits. Look for the choices behind a public image.

- [Octodrachm: Head of Arsinoe II (obverse); Double Cornucopia (reverse)](https://clevelandart.org/art/1965.552) — 205–145 BCE; Cleveland Museum of Art, 1965.552.
- [Tetradrachm: Head of Alexander III [The Great] (obverse); Athena (reverse)](https://clevelandart.org/art/1916.994) — 306 BCE; Cleveland Museum of Art, 1916.994.

**Observe · 2 minutes.** Describe one visible detail on each coin. Compare portraits, lettering, and symbols before assigning them a meaning.

**Compare · 3 minutes.** Read the dates and materials. What might the portraits communicate about authority? Separate what the museum record establishes from your interpretation.

**Test the limit · 3 minutes.** Could either coin establish how a scholarly community was funded? Name one additional source you would need.

**Return to the reading.** In the Alexandria lesson, read Strabo’s account of the Mouseion. Distinguish what his account says about support from what the coins show about royal imagery.

### The Odyssey: What changes when a story is retold?

[Open the comparison](http://localhost:5173/collections?study=odyssey-retold) · 8 minutes

An ancient flask and a later painting open different windows onto myth.

- [Siren-Shaped Perfume Flask](https://clevelandart.org/art/1928.193) — 600–480 BCE; Cleveland Museum of Art, 1928.193.
- [Hermes Ordering Calypso to Release Odysseus](https://clevelandart.org/art/1992.2) — c. 1670; Cleveland Museum of Art, 1992.2.

**Observe · 2 minutes.** Describe the flask’s form and one visible action in the painting. Keep visual description separate from the identities supplied by their titles.

**Compare · 3 minutes.** Compare their dates, materials, and object types. How might a perfume flask and a painting invite different encounters with a story?

**Test the limit · 3 minutes.** Neither object is an eyewitness account of Odysseus’s travels. What would you need to read before claiming that either illustrates a particular passage?

**Return to the reading.** Return to your assigned Odyssey passage. The Sirens and Calypso belong outside Book IX; use this pair to discuss reception and the limits of a visual analogy, not as illustrations of the Cyclops episode.

### Jane Austen: What do appearances leave out?

[Open the comparison](http://localhost:5173/collections?study=austen-first-impressions) · 8 minutes

A dress and a teapot invite questions about first impressions and social encounters.

- [Dress](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/90487) — ca. 1810; The Metropolitan Museum of Art, 13.49.16.
- [Teapot](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/192043) — ca. 1800; The Metropolitan Museum of Art, 13.31.1a, b.

**Observe · 2 minutes.** Describe a detail of the dress and a detail of the teapot. What is visible, and what did you learn only by reading their museum records?

**Compare · 3 minutes.** Imagine the questions an observer might ask about clothing and hospitality. Which assumptions about a person could these objects tempt you to make without enough evidence?

**Test the limit · 3 minutes.** Neither record identifies an Austen character or Austen herself as an owner. What can the objects help you ask, and what can only the reading help you answer?

**Return to the reading.** Return to the assigned Pride and Prejudice passage. Find a judgment about someone, then identify the textual evidence that supports or challenges it. Keep the museum comparison distinct from that evidence.

### Macbeth: Who shapes our judgment of a character?

[Open the comparison](http://localhost:5173/collections?study=macbeth-performance) · 8 minutes

A letter-reading scene and a theatrical satire reveal the choices behind a performance.

- [Lady Macbeth (Shakespeare, Macbeth, Act 1, Scene 5)](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/707811) — first published 1800; reissued 1852; The Metropolitan Museum of Art, 42.119.538.
- [An English Country Performance of Macbeth](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/713071) — 1790; The Metropolitan Museum of Art, 17.3.2601.

**Observe · 2 minutes.** Describe the letter and pose in the Lady Macbeth print. Then find a detail that reveals how the country performance is being made.

**Compare · 3 minutes.** Compare how each print directs attention: toward one character’s response, or toward performers and an audience. How might those choices change a viewer’s judgment?

**Test the limit · 3 minutes.** Read the dates and the satire’s description. Neither print records Shakespeare’s original staging. Identify one interpretation you must test against the play’s words.

**Return to the reading.** Return to the assigned Macbeth passage. Identify a speaker’s attempt to influence another person, quote the relevant words, and explain what the print adds or leaves out. Act 1, Scene 5 is the letter scene; do not treat it as an illustration of every assigned scene.

### The Tempest: Who gets the viewer’s sympathy?

[Open the comparison](http://localhost:5173/collections?study=tempest-perspectives) · 8 minutes

A portrait-like Miranda and a grouped encounter frame the island’s story differently.

- [Miranda (Shakespeare, The Tempest, Act 1, Scene 2)](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/431240) — 1784; The Metropolitan Museum of Art, 53.600.4489.
- [Scene from the Tempest: Caliban, Prospero and Miranda](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/407873) — 1783–87, reissued 1801; The Metropolitan Museum of Art, 53.638.19.

**Observe · 2 minutes.** Describe Miranda’s pose in the single-figure print and the distances between figures in the grouped scene. Separate visible detail from names supplied by the records.

**Compare · 3 minutes.** Which figure does each composition invite you to identify with? Identify a visual choice behind that response, including how Caliban is framed.

**Test the limit · 3 minutes.** These are later interpretations. Explain why an artist’s threatening or sympathetic portrayal cannot settle the competing claims made by the play’s speakers.

**Return to the reading.** Return to your assigned Tempest passage and name who speaks each account of the island’s past. Support a comparison with the words, keeping the later artists’ judgments distinct from textual evidence.

### Declaration of Independence: How does an argument become a public memory?

[Open the comparison](http://localhost:5173/collections?study=declaration-paper) · 8 minutes

A writing tool and a retrospective print connect making an argument with remembering it.

- [Inkstand](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/4435) — 1700–1800; The Metropolitan Museum of Art, 30.120.149.
- [Signing the Declaration of Independence](https://clevelandart.org/art/1919.675) — 1823; Cleveland Museum of Art, 1919.675.

**Observe · 2 minutes.** Describe a practical feature of the inkstand and the arrangement of people and papers in the etching.

**Compare · 3 minutes.** One object supports writing; the other represents a political event. What can each help you ask about the work and presentation of public argument?

**Test the limit · 3 minutes.** The inkstand’s date and place are broad, and the etching is from 1823. Explain why neither establishes the signing date of a particular person or equal access to political rights.

**Return to the reading.** Return to the Declaration’s assigned claims and grievances. Distinguish the argument it makes from evidence about its drafting, signing, or later implementation; the two museum objects do not supply all three.

### Frankenstein: Does understanding a system mean controlling it?

[Open the comparison](http://localhost:5173/collections?study=frankenstein-models) · 8 minutes

An orrery and an anatomical drawing invite a distinction between representing and creating.

- [Orrery clock](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/785846) — 1790; The Metropolitan Museum of Art, 2019.283.73.
- [Anatomical drawing](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/384401) — 18th century; The Metropolitan Museum of Art, 54.580.2.

**Observe · 2 minutes.** Describe a feature of the orrery’s arrangement and a choice of line or shading in the anatomical drawing.

**Compare · 3 minutes.** What does a mechanical model or a drawing make easier to study? Name something each representation leaves out of the system it depicts.

**Test the limit · 3 minutes.** Neither object is documented as Shelley’s or Victor’s. Explain why representing bodily form or celestial motion does not demonstrate an ability to create life.

**Return to the reading.** Return to the assigned Frankenstein passage. Identify a claim about knowledge, ambition, or responsibility and test it with the speaker’s words. Use the objects as a comparison, not as proof that the fictional experiment could work.

### Charles Dickens: What does a second look reveal?

[Open the comparison](http://localhost:5173/collections?study=dickens-second-life) · 8 minutes

A preserved dress and a later clothes shop shift attention from appearance to reuse.

- [Morning dress](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/108064) — 1840–45; The Metropolitan Museum of Art, 1977.91.2.
- [An Old Clothes Shop, Seven Dials](https://clevelandart.org/art/2019.51) — 1877; Cleveland Museum of Art, 2019.51.

**Observe · 2 minutes.** Describe one feature of the dress and one visible detail of the shop photograph without inventing a wearer’s or shopper’s biography.

**Compare · 3 minutes.** How does looking at a garment in isolation differ from looking at clothes among people and goods? Which initial assumptions become less secure?

**Test the limit · 3 minutes.** Read the dates: the dress is 1840–45 and the photograph is 1877. Neither links the two objects to one owner or documents a scene from A Christmas Carol.

**Return to the reading.** Return to the assigned A Christmas Carol passage. Find a moment when a person is judged or reconsidered, and explain the textual detail that changes the judgment. The later photograph is a comparison, not a picture of Dickens’s fictional event.

### Frederick Douglass: What can a portrait say that testimony cannot?

[Open the comparison](http://localhost:5173/collections?study=douglass-public-image) · 8 minutes

Two portraits of Douglass invite close comparison with his account of learning to read.

- [Frederick Douglass](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/282066) — ca. 1855; The Metropolitan Museum of Art, 2001.756.
- [Frederick Douglass](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/286586) — 1876; The Metropolitan Museum of Art, 2005.100.754.

**Observe · 2 minutes.** Describe a detail of pose, clothing, or framing in each portrait. Read the records to separate observation from the sitter’s identification.

**Compare · 3 minutes.** What changes between the ca. 1855 daguerreotype and the 1876 photograph? What questions about audience and public identity do the differences invite?

**Test the limit · 3 minutes.** Both portraits postdate the 1845 Narrative. Name a claim about Douglass’s literacy experience or thoughts that neither portrait can establish.

**Return to the reading.** Return to Douglass’s assigned account of literacy. Explain what his first-person testimony contributes that a portrait cannot, using a specific passage rather than treating expression as evidence of thought.

### Seneca Falls: What does one learner’s work prove?

[Open the comparison](http://localhost:5173/collections?study=seneca-learning) · 8 minutes

Two named makers’ samplers make skill visible while leaving broader opportunity open to investigation.

- [Sampler](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/14094) — 1830; The Metropolitan Museum of Art, 1984.331.15.
- [Sampler](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/19997) — 1844; The Metropolitan Museum of Art, 2008.453.

**Observe · 2 minutes.** Describe lettering, repeated stitches, or imagery in each sampler. Identify each maker and date from the museum records.

**Compare · 3 minutes.** Compare what Mary Ann Stauffer’s 1830 work and Mariah Boil’s 1844 work reveal about practiced skills. Keep their different individual and community contexts visible.

**Test the limit · 3 minutes.** Explain why evidence that these makers learned particular skills neither proves nor disproves a national claim about access to higher education.

**Return to the reading.** Return to a specific educational grievance in the Declaration of Sentiments. State its scope precisely and identify an additional source that could evaluate it. Neither sampler documents participation at the convention.
